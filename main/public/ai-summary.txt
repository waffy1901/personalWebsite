Kubernetes Autoscaling for Transaction-Critical Services:
* URL: https://waffy.dev/case-studies/kubernetes-autoscaling/
* Organization: The Home Depot
* Timeframe: 2025
* Summary: Validated and deployed Horizontal Pod Autoscaling (HPA) for a
  high-throughput service previously capped at static capacity, improving
  latency, errors, throughput, and CPU efficiency under production traffic.
* Metrics: 40% lower mean latency; 89% fewer errors; 34.8M weekly requests;
  26% lower average CPU
* 40% lower mean latency: Mean latency moved from 121 ms to 72 ms after
  rollout.
* 89% fewer errors: Reported 89% reduction; error count versus rate remains
  unconfirmed.
* 34.8M weekly requests: Throughput increased from 24.9M to 34.8M requests per
  week.
* 26% lower average CPU: Average CPU usage fell while dynamic scaling expanded
  capacity.
* Stack: Kubernetes, Horizontal Pod Autoscaling, GCP, Grafana,
  Prometheus/PromQL, k6, Postman
* Problem:
The service supported transaction-critical traffic with a fixed 20-pod
deployment. HPA had not actually been enabled: its minimum and maximum replica
counts were equal, so it could not change capacity in response to demand.

The failure mode was capacity exhaustion. When demand exceeded what those
replicas could serve, the fixed ceiling prevented HPA from adding capacity;
latency and errors were the service signals to examine alongside resource
pressure.

* Ownership and validation:
A reliability engineering director asked for HPA to be enabled urgently. I
tested over a weekend and concluded from those tests that enabling HPA could
have avoided an outage. That is a test-informed counterfactual, not proof that
a production outage was prevented.

I deployed and validated the change, using CPU as the initial scaling signal
and adding memory later. I compared the baseline with subsequent production
observations in Grafana, looking at latency, errors, request throughput, and
average CPU together.

* Decision and tradeoff:
Keeping equal replica bounds makes capacity predictable, but leaves demand
spikes dependent on manual capacity changes. Allowing HPA to vary replicas
gives the service room to respond to resource pressure, at the cost of a
larger and changing resource footprint. The production range moved to 50-100
pods rather than the previous fixed 20.

This was a bounded capacity decision: even dynamic scaling cannot add replicas
beyond its maximum. Demand that exceeds the capacity available at that bound
can still exhaust the service. Scaling adds headroom; it does not guarantee
that every bottleneck or outage cause is removed.

* How bounded autoscaling changes capacity: Workload demand creates resource
  pressure. CPU, with memory added later, informs HPA's replica decision
  within minimum and maximum bounds. The resulting service capacity affects
  latency, errors, and throughput, which I compared in Grafana.
1. Workload demand: Transaction requests place demand on the available service
   capacity.
Demand creates resource pressure: Workload demand to Resource signals.
2. Resource signals: CPU was the initial scaling signal; memory was added
   later.

within bounds.
3. HPA decision within bounds: Equal bounds prevent capacity changes. A 50-100
   range permits replica changes within that limit.

   to serve demand.

* Outcome:
After rollout, mean latency moved from 121 ms to 72 ms (reported as 40%
lower), weekly requests increased from 24.9M to 34.8M, and average CPU usage
decreased 26%. The existing result also reports 89% fewer errors, but whether
that measure is an error count or a normalized rate remains unconfirmed.

These are observed baseline-versus-comparison results alongside the capacity
change. Request volume also changed, so the comparison does not isolate HPA as
the sole cause of every improvement.

* Evidence and limits:
The measurements came from Grafana. I recall averages over approximately
30-day baseline and comparison periods; exact dates and the error measure's
denominator are not confirmed. The weekly request figures describe throughput
and should not be treated as those periods' total request counts.

I do not have confirmed stabilization settings, rollback checks, or team
handoff details to share. This account therefore supports the testing,
deployment, and observed comparison, but does not establish a complete
recovery procedure or independently verified outage prevention.

* Lesson:
The first check is whether autoscaling has room to act: a configured HPA with
equal bounds still leaves the service at fixed capacity. The next check is
service behavior across multiple signals and changing traffic, with
measurement limits stated alongside the results.

JFrog OIDC and CI Credential Lifecycle:
* URL: https://waffy.dev/case-studies/jfrog-oidc-adoption/
* Organization: The Home Depot
* Timeframe: October 2026
* Summary: Drove a move from shared Maven and Docker credentials to
  short-lived, job-scoped authentication across two teams and approximately 20
  repositories.
* Metrics: 2 teams adoption scope; ~20 repositories in scope; 1,100+ reported
  scanning findings resolved
* 2 teams adoption scope: Owner-reported October 2026 JFrog authentication
  work.
* ~20 repositories in scope: Maven and Docker CI authentication moved away
  from shared credentials.
* 1,100+ reported scanning findings resolved: Findings tied to plaintext token
  output in CI logs across approximately 20 repositories; not a count of
  distinct credentials.
* Stack: JFrog, OIDC, Maven, Docker, CI/CD, Short-lived credentials
* Problem and ownership:
Shared Maven and Docker credentials crossed repository and job boundaries. The
owner reported plaintext token output in CI logs across approximately 20
repositories, linked to more than 1,100 scanning findings. The finding count
describes scanner results, not 1,100 distinct leaked secrets.

I drove JFrog OIDC adoption across two teams in October 2026, including CI
pipeline adaptations for short-lived, job-scoped authentication. My role was
to drive that cross-team change; participating repository work was shared.

* Decision and trust boundary:
The alternative I considered was a service account and password with custom
masking. I chose OIDC and short-lived job-scoped tokens: masking addresses
output handling, while job identity and temporary authentication also change
how credentials are distributed and how long they remain usable. This made the
identity-to-access boundary part of pipeline reliability.

The tradeoff is that artifact operations now depend on the OIDC exchange and
its trust configuration. A failed exchange can block a build even when the
application code is healthy. The public account omits private issuer,
audience, subject, trust-policy, permission, and token-lifetime settings; the
diagram illustrates the boundary without supplying deployable provider
configuration.

* Job identity to artifact access: Illustrative trust sequence only. Internal
  provider policies, permission values, and exchange endpoints are
  intentionally omitted.
1. CI job identity: A job presents identity for an OIDC-based authentication
   flow.
Identity is evaluated at the trust boundary: CI job identity to JFrog
authentication boundary.
2. JFrog authentication boundary: Trust configuration determines whether the
   identity can obtain artifact access.
Accepted identity enables scoped authentication: JFrog authentication boundary
to Short-lived job token.
3. Short-lived job token: The job uses temporary authentication for its Maven
   or Docker artifact operations.
An accepted exchange, a successful artifact operation, and absence of
plaintext token output are separate verification concerns.

* Verification and recovery limits:
The work included CI adaptations and staged verification of Maven/Docker
authentication. Authentication and artifact-operation checks serve different
purposes: accepting an identity is not, by itself, evidence that the job can
complete its required artifact operation. Short-lived tokens also do not make
printing tokens safe.

A future rollout should document recovery from a failed exchange without
reintroducing shared secrets or logging token material. That is a recommended
improvement; the historical recovery procedure is not documented here.

* Illustrative credential-boundary checklist: Conceptual review aid, not JFrog
  API syntax, internal CI configuration, or a historical test log. It contains
  no usable credentials or policy values.
Authentication and use
job identity -> trust evaluation
accepted exchange -> short-lived job token
job token -> Maven / Docker operation

Separate evidence questions
Can the intended job authenticate?
Can its artifact operation complete?
Does output avoid plaintext token material?
What does each scanner finding count?

* Outcome and lesson:
The owner reports adoption across two teams and approximately 20 repositories,
replacing shared credentials with short-lived, job-scoped tokens and resolving
more than 1,100 findings tied to plaintext token output in CI logs. The scan
tool, observation window, and deduplication method are unspecified; the
finding count is not a distinct-secret count or a numerical measure of risk
reduction.

I was the first to adopt this approach within my engineering organization and
established it as the standard there. I reinforced that direction in a later
engineer's migration PR review, explaining the implementation already in place
and the rationale before approving. The reported implementation scope remains
two teams and approximately 20 repositories; this is not a company-wide
standard claim.

The lesson was to treat credential lifetime, job identity, pipeline usability,
and logging behavior as related boundaries. A future evidence package should
preserve redacted artifact-operation results and repeatable scan definitions
so adoption and exposure reduction can be assessed independently.

Intended wider workflow (not all demonstrated):
1. Jira story: Start the wider workflow with one skill call and a story's
   requirements.
2. Preflight and discovery: Discover repository context and prerequisites
   before proposing work.
3. Repository analysis and planning: Turn the story and code context into an
   implementation plan.
4. Implementation and source PR: Merge source changes with at least two human
   approvals before deployment intake.
5. Docker build: A person dispatches the build; the poller discovers its run
   and reads the image tag.
6. Google Artifact Registry publication: The build publishes its container
   image to Google Artifact Registry.
7. GKE delivery validation: Promote through dev, qp, and qa with configured
   reviews and regional health/version checks.
8. Draft production PR: Open an image-tag proposal for an app owner to review
   before production merge and rollout.
Technical decisions:
* Used a scheduled state machine, one run-record writer, and persisted steps
  to avoid depending on an open agent session.
* Checked source approvals on the merged head and limited cdk8s changes to the
  service's image tag.
* Required an explicit live flag and a non-dry run before writes; the poller
  never approves, merges, or marks PRs ready.
* Separated application-code push-back from cdk8s configuration fixes, which
  require a new reviewable PR.
Limitations:
* This October 27-29, 2026 hackathon prototype demonstrated one GKE service,
  ending at a draft production PR closed unmerged, with no production rollout.
* Build dispatch and four polls were manual. Full Jira-to-implementation
  orchestration and unattended completion were not demonstrated.
* Run records were persisted, but recovery across an agent-session restart was
  not tested; one session drove the live run.
* Dev lacked code-owner review; qp and qa received human code-owner approval.
  Dev Slack approval messages still needed correction.
* Public notes are sanitized; employer code, repository links, internal URLs,
  and screenshots are not published.
